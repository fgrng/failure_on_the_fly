# [Titel offen]

Fabian Grünig · Markus Vogel

## Introduction

A student says something and the teacher has a few seconds. The answer may be a slip, or it may be
the visible end of a rule the student has been applying for months. The question the teacher asks
next either brings that rule out or does not, and the lesson moves on either way.

Learning to do this is part of learning to teach mathematics. Teacher education brings practice
into a course through vignettes, which show a classroom situation without its time pressure. What
a vignette cannot do is answer. It was assembled before the pre-service teachers arrived and stays
as it is whatever they ask, so the half of the work that consists of asking is not there to be
practised. Role play answers, but then the student is whatever the peer playing the role makes of
it.

We built Failure on the Fly for that missing half. Pre-service teachers put their own questions to
a simulated student, a language model instructed to apply one error pattern, and the vignette
settles which pattern before anyone starts talking.

Section 2 sets out what on-the-fly assessment requires, which is that the teacher bring a
student's thinking out rather than collect it. Section 3 examines what case work can and cannot
offer for that, and Section 4 whether a simulated student holds an error under questioning.
Section 5 presents the application with one worked vignette from descriptive statistics, Section 6
the research question and Section 7 the design.

This is a theoretical and developmental paper. It offers an argument, a tool derived from it and a
study now under way, without findings yet. Data collection runs from autumn 2026 to spring 2027, and we will
report the first findings at the conference.

## On-the-fly assessment

Much of what teachers judge in a classroom is not finished work. Ruiz-Primo and Furtak (2007)
describe what happens during a lesson instead as a cycle they call ESRU. The teacher elicits, the
student responds, the teacher recognises the response, and the teacher uses what it revealed
(p. 61). Their choice of the first word is deliberate. In formal assessment a teacher gathers
information that already exists, whereas eliciting means "evoking, educing, bringing out, or
developing" (p. 58). The thinking the teacher needs is not lying there waiting to be collected. A
question has to bring it out.

This gives the cycle a property that decides how it can be learned. Each step conditions the next
one in both directions. What the teacher asks depends on what the student has just said, and what
the student says depends on what the teacher has just asked. The diagnosis is not formed before
the conversation and then confirmed in it. It is built inside the conversation, and a question
that turns out to be badly aimed costs a turn that does not come back.

What the teacher is after is usually not an isolated slip. Prediger (2008) shows for the
multiplication of fractions that a persistent student conception can be a rule rather than a
lapse, carried over from a domain where it held into one where it no longer does. She locates the
obstacle in the structure of the mathematical content rather than in the individual learner
(pp. 12–13). A rule of that kind stays invisible for as long as no question makes it apply. So the
demand on the teacher is not to recognise an error pattern lying in plain view. The demand is to
ask the question that makes it show.

Research on teacher noticing describes part of this work and is explicit about where it stops.
Jacobs et al. (2010) conceptualise professional noticing of children's mathematical thinking as
three related skills, attending to children's strategies, interpreting their understandings, and
deciding how to respond, and they place these in the in-the-moment decision making of the
classroom (pp. 172–173). They then set the response itself outside the construct, which leaves
them, in their own words, focusing on "intended responding" (p. 173). Van Es and Sherin (2002)
developed their account of noticing through video analysis, and they count it an advantage that
video lets teachers "remove themselves from the demands of the classroom, such as having to take
action and make instructional decisions in the moment" (p. 576). They name the effect of noticing
on instruction itself as a question for later work (pp. 592–593). Leuders et al. (2020) draw the
same line from the other side. Their DiaCoM framework models diagnostic judgement as information
processing, and it leaves out the actions teachers take around the judgement on purpose, among
them the conduct of diagnostic conversations, so that the cognitive processes can be studied under
experimental control (p. 496).

None of these boundaries is an oversight and each has a good reason behind it. What matters here
is that they fall in the same place. All three stop just before the point at which the teacher has
to act and then live with what comes back, and that point is where eliciting happens. A learning
opportunity for on-the-fly assessment therefore has to let pre-service teachers ask their own
questions, and it has to answer them. This is the standard we apply in what follows.

## Case work and its limits

Practice-based teacher education has a vocabulary for how practice gets into a course. McDonald et
al. (2013) organise the pedagogy of teacher education as a cycle. Candidates first meet a practice
through modelling, video analysis or case analysis, three pedagogies they group together as
representations of practice. They then plan and rehearse it in a protected setting, an
approximation of practice. They then enact it with real students, and afterwards they analyse what
they did (pp. 383–384). The core practice they work the cycle out for is eliciting students'
thinking, which they gloss as drawing out students' ideas and responding to those ideas in ways
that move learning forward (p. 383). That is the practice at issue here.

A vignette is a representation, and it earns its place. Wirth et al. (2023) give pre-service
primary teachers a photograph of a child's work, a teacher's handwritten observation notes and a
comic strip of the exchange between child and teacher, and ask them to diagnose the child's
arithmetic. The situation is deliberately less complex than a real lesson. Less information is
present, the pressure of time and of having to act is absent, and guiding questions point at what
matters (p. 369). The reduction is the design and not a compromise. It lets a novice think about a
diagnostic situation instead of surviving one.

The limit of a representation is not a matter of degree. Wirth et al. remark of their own vignette
that the information in it was gathered and recorded by someone else (p. 371). That is what a
representation is. Whoever put the questions to the child put them before the pre-service teacher
arrived, and the case is closed before the learner's first question. Some of what this costs can
be designed back in. Hoppe et al. (2024) wanted their video test to
keep the fleetingness of a real classroom, so they let participants pause the video to take notes
but not watch it again (p. 48). Responsiveness cannot be restored that way, because it is not a
property of the material. It needs someone to answer. In the terms of Section 2, attending and
interpreting have somewhere to happen, and deciding how to respond ends where Jacobs et al. said it
ends, at intent.

The field's own answer to this is the approximation, and the research on it shows the price.
Kavanagh et al. (2019) studied a programme in which early-career teachers rehearsed leading
discussions while their peers played the students, and they asked whether an approximation can be
used to practise responsiveness rather than a repertoire of moves. It can. But the approximations
that stayed tightly on responsiveness were also the most tightly constrained, because the teacher
educators had settled the text, the task and the goal in advance so that nothing else competed for
attention. The teacher educators rehearsed with each other beforehand in order to run them. Every
rehearsal ran in a small group with a teacher educator present or nearby. This is a defensible use
of a teacher educator's time and it does not scale.

A second cost matters more for research. When a peer plays the student, the student is whatever
that peer makes of the role. Some participants in this programme were told to take on the persona
of a child they knew and others to be themselves. What each teacher then had to respond to was
different, which is no problem for rehearsal and disqualifying for measurement. The two formats
divide the demand between them. A representation holds the case still and cannot answer. An
approximation answers and cannot hold the case still, and it needs a person for every conversation.

What Section 2 asks for is an approximation in which the error pattern is fixed. Kavanagh et al.
also name the doubt that comes with it. For a skill like responding to students, they write, "the
presence of actual students offers opportunities to learn that may not be able to be simulated in
less authentic environments where students are not present" (p. 105). We treat that as the question
to ask rather than as a settled objection, and we return to it in Section 6.

## Simulated students

Heitzmann et al. (2019) define the kind of simulation that matters here. It is a learning
environment in which a segment of reality is presented so that diagnostic activities become
possible, and in which "learners' actions influence the further development of the system" (p. 9).
The second condition is the one Section 3 found missing, and it is what makes a simulation an
approximation rather than a representation (p. 9).

Simulated classrooms are not new in teacher education. Fischer et al. (2021) had pre-service
biology teachers put questions to virtual students who answered according to a preset ability
profile. Their participants judged whether an answer was scientifically correct in 91% of cases and
identified the specific misconception behind it in 59%. Naming the misconception is the harder
half, and it is the half a conversation is for. What a preset profile cannot do is answer a
question nobody anticipated.

A language model can. It can also be told which error to make, so that the diagnostic object stays
the same across participants, which is the control Section 3 found approximations lacking. Holding
both halves at once is the claim, and the evidence for it is mixed.

The relevant evidence is discouraging. Kortenkamp and Larkin (2026) had a language
model solve mathematics word problems and found that its wrong answers did resemble students'
answers. When they then intervened as a teacher would, the pedagogically specific prompts were
routinely ignored while a content-free "Are you sure?" worked. They conclude that a model may be
suited to modelling the solutions students produce and much less to modelling how students respond
to instruction. Martynova et al. (2025) had teachers tutor simulated students and found them too
ready to agree, giving up an approach as soon as it was questioned, where a real student digs in.
Chen et al. (2026) put a number on the gap. Across nine models, accuracy fell from 66% on solving
mathematics problems to 40% on predicting what a student holding a given misconception would answer
next.

That gap sits exactly where a diagnostic conversation lives. The error has to survive being
questioned, and being questioned is what these studies find the simulation does not survive. Two
things follow for the design. The error pattern belongs in the vignette and not in a prompt written
on the day, and holding it is an engineering problem rather than a matter of asking politely.
Whether it can be held well enough for pre-service teachers to accept the counterpart is not
something argument can settle.

## Failure on the Fly

Failure on the Fly is a web application in which a participant holds a diagnostic conversation
with a simulated student. A session opens with a framing narrative. The participant is visiting a
class, an experienced teacher introduces the situation, and the participant then sits down with
one student. The narrative situates the conversation, and without that situating the
approximation to practice does not hold. Every vignette specifies exactly one error pattern and
holds it fixed, so the diagnostic object is the same for every participant. That is what makes a
vignette an instrument rather than a case. Each
vignette also carries a task context, a learning task and the student's exercise book, so the
conversation starts from work already done, as it does in a classroom. A turn limit ends the
conversation and we do not show participants how much of it is left, which keeps a measured
amount of the pressure of the real situation. The session closes with a debrief in which the
experienced teacher asks for a diagnostic account. We record that account and do not score it,
which keeps the format a rehearsal rather than a test. The simulation core is subject-agnostic.
The mathematics sits in the vignette.

One of the [N] vignettes may stand for the rest. Lea is in grade 8. She has worked on two
boxplots showing the heights of the players in two basketball teams of 20 players each, shown in
Figure 1. She was asked which team has the greater range, how many players in Team B are taller
than 1.98 m, and in which team more players lie between the lower and the upper quartile. She
gives the range correctly. She answers the other two from the size of the box, about three
players above 1.98 m because the whisker above the box is short, and Team B for the last question
because its box is wide. Lea reads frequency from area. Heursen et al. (2025) describe this error
as an incomplete conceptual change from bar and pie charts, where area is indeed proportional to
frequency, to the boxplot, which is a density display whose box holds the middle 50% whatever its
width. Lea reads all five summary values correctly and can state the definition of a quartile.
The error surfaces only when someone asks how many, which is what makes the vignette a diagnostic
task and not a puzzle.

The exchange below is one we wrote to show what the vignette specifies. It is not a record of a
session, and no participant has yet played this vignette. P marks the participant and S the
simulated student. We wrote it in German, the language of the study, and have translated it here.
The German original is available from the authors.

**P** How many players lie between the lower and the upper quartile?

**S** For Team B about 14 or 15. The box takes up almost everything. For Team A more like eight.

**P** You just said quartile. What is a quartile again?

**S** A quarter. At the lower quartile a quarter is below it, and at the upper one a quarter is
above.

**P** If a quarter lies below the lower quartile and a quarter above the upper one, how many lie
in between?

**S** Then it would be half. But the box for Team A is much narrower, so there cannot be as many
in it as for Team B. I think half is right when the box is normally wide. For Team B it is wider,
so there are more.

A static case would have shown the written work and the two wrong answers. It could not have
shown the third turn, in which Lea states the definition of a quartile correctly and keeps her
area rule anyway. That turn exists only because the participant asked for the definition, and a
fixed case has no way of offering it, whoever reads it.

## Research question

The argument so far rests on a premise we have not tested. A simulated student made of text is
only worth talking to if pre-service teachers accept it as a counterpart. If they do not, they
solve a puzzle instead of holding a diagnostic conversation, and the responsiveness we have
gained buys nothing. Perceived quality is therefore a condition for any later claim about
learning. We ask how pre-service teachers perceive the quality of dynamic vignettes with a
simulated student. The question has two parts. Do they experience the simulated student and the
situation as authentic, and does the error pattern come across as a systematic misconception
rather than a slip? And do they experience the conversation as responsive to what they ask,
while the environment carries the situation rather than demanding attention of its own? We ask
this of the vignettes and not of the participants, because the answer has to tell us which
vignettes to keep. Asking instead whether the vignettes are suitable would promise evidence of
effectiveness that this design cannot give.

## Study design

We will conduct the study at Heidelberg University of Education in Germany and St. Gallen
University of Teacher Education in Switzerland. Participants are pre-service teachers preparing
to teach mathematics at secondary level. The sample is a convenience sample. We asked teacher
educators whether they wanted to try the vignettes in their courses, and those who agreed
contributed their seminar group. Most participants are in the bachelor phase and most seminars
are compulsory, but neither holds throughout. Seminars have between 10 and 25 students. Group
membership is recorded for each seminar and each institution, so we can describe distributions
by group, but the study is not designed as a comparison between the two countries. Data
collection will run from autumn to spring and has not yet started, so we report no participant
numbers.

Participants will reach the study through a link and work without a user account. They will
consent to taking part and, separately, to the processing of audio. Those who withhold the
second consent can do everything by keyboard. Research data are held under a pseudonymous token,
separate from any user account. Each participant will then work through one to three vignettes
from a pool of [N]. A session consists of the framing narrative, the diagnostic conversation
with the simulated student, and a written diagnostic account. A turn limit closes the
conversation, and participants are not shown how much of it is left. The vignettes cover the
content areas of the participating seminars, among them descriptive statistics as well as
arithmetic.

The questionnaire covers four dimensions. Contextual authenticity draws on the vignette criteria
of authenticity, typicality and representativeness (von Aufschnaiter et al., 2017, as cited in
Wirth et al., 2023). Responsiveness asks whether the simulated student took up what the
participant had said, the contingency between eliciting and responding that the ESRU cycle
describes (Ruiz-Primo & Furtak, 2007). Extraneous cognitive load and immersion are adapted from
Syring et al. (2015) and Hoppe et al. (2024). We administer the authenticity and responsiveness
items after each session, so they refer to a single vignette. We administer cognitive load and
immersion once at the end, where they refer to the environment as a whole. All items use a
six-point Likert scale from 1 (do not agree at all) to 6 (completely agree), with no neutral
midpoint that would let participants avoid a judgement. We administer the items in German. They
are adapted rather than taken over as validated scales, and the change of response format rules
out comparison with published values. One responsiveness item, translated by the authors, reads
"The student's answers followed on from what I had asked". [ITEM]

Our unit of analysis is the vignette and not the person. At item level we will report medians,
interquartile ranges and the frequency across the six categories, treating the responses as
ordinal. Before forming a score per dimension we will report McDonald's omega for it,
descriptively, because the items are adapted. For authenticity and responsiveness we will report
one row per vignette with the number of participants who played it. A value pooled across
vignettes is secondary, because a participant enters it up to three times. Cognitive load and
immersion stay at the level of the person. Differences between vignettes are confounded with the
seminar, since different seminars work on different content areas, so we will describe them and
not test them. We report no power analysis, because we test no hypothesis. Transcripts and
diagnostic accounts are collected but not analysed here.

## Outlook

Data collection runs from autumn to spring, so questionnaire results will be available before the
conference. We will report them by vignette, with the number of participants behind each figure,
and we will say which vignettes were not accepted as readily as which were. A vignette that fails
to work as a counterpart is the most useful finding the study can produce, because it tells us
what to change.

We will also bring conversations. The exchange in Section 5 is one we wrote. By February we expect
transcripts of sessions that pre-service teachers held themselves, and these show what a
questionnaire cannot, namely what they asked and how the simulated student answered.

Two questions stay outside this study. Whether the participants' diagnostic accounts are accurate
belongs to diagnostic quality, and whether the practice improves anything belongs to
effectiveness. Both depend on the answer to the present one.
